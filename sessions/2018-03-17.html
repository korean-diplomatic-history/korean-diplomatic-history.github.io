Reviewer(s)' Comments to Author:

Reviewer: 1

Comments to the Author
This is a very interesting paper which has the potential to make a number of useful contributions to a variety of debates in IR including on the historical turn, civilizations and civilizational standards, world order, critiques of Euro-centrism and American hegemony and rising powers. As it stands though, none of these contributions are really made apparent. Not only, but the paper also problematically lacks in analytical depth and conceptual precision. All of these issues, which I will outline in greater detail below, need to be seriously addressed before the paper can be considered ready for publication in my view. I therefore recommend a quite hefty process of revision.

1) Argument and contribution
I struggled to identify clearly the paper's argument. The author puts forward a variety of different claims in this regard, for instance that the focus is on the US and China's "contestation over the global norm or the standard of civilization regarding the question of world governance". Elsewhere the paper claims that its intent is to "explore the constitutive processes of self-identity formation of America and China against the European Others (the Westphalian system and imperialism), thereby demonstrating the two states' exceptionalist doctrines and their alternative visions of a postmodern world order". Finally I also gathered from the conclusion that the paper's aim was to reveal how "the two revolutionary states are more likely to conflict each other by unilaterally stressing their own standard of civilization".
Which one of these is the paper's intention? Is it to examine US and China's visions of 'world governance'? Yet the paper hardly addresses the issue of 'world governance' throughout (by the way what exactly is 'world governance'? Does the author mean 'global governance'? Or maybe 'world order'?). Is its concern to show how America and China's visions and approaches to international order are distinct from Europe's (here I have some strong reservations regarding the concept of 'postmodern world order' which I will touch upon later on in my comments). Or its interest in demonstrating how a rising China will inevitably clash with the US if a dialogue of civilizations view does not supplant their own exceptionalist civilizational projects. Yet, again, the issue of impending clash between these two powers is hardly addressed throughout the paper.
The above problems are compounded by the fact that the paper does not connect explicitly any one particular debate in IR. In other words, who is the author speaking to? And, following on that, what exactly is its contribution then? Is the manuscript speaking to those pushing for a historical turn in IR, to debates about civilizations and civilizational standards, to discussions about world order and rising powers, or critiques of Euro-centrism in the discipline? Answering this question is important also because it would focus more clearly the paper's argument and its contribution to on-going discussions in the discipline.

2) Analysis VS Historical Description
I did appreciate the historical orientation of the paper. Yet, I must admit, that I did struggle quite a bit throughout the paper's two main parts that recover the American/Philadelphian and Chinese/Tianxian post-Westphalian visions of world order. Both sections are overwhelmingly descriptive and narrative, I had a very hard time to understand what exactly these two visions of world order entailed and why and how were these 'post-Westphalian'. Both sections should be much more analytical, organized around a number of key concepts and ideas – without necessarily foregoing the historical dimension by tracing them back to particular historical events or long-standing intellectual currents – in order for the reader to grasp exactly what is unique and important about these two visions.

3) Civilization or Civilizations?
The paper seems to mix two different, although not necessarily mutually exclusive, ways to think about civilization/s in world politics. This confusion also detracts from the argument the paper seeks to make. I was not entirely clear whether the paper argued that the vision animating America and China's view of world order was driven by different civilizational backgrounds (i.e. a view of civilizations in the plural) or by the desire to set new/different standards of civilizations (i.e. a view of civilization in the singular). This confusion is captured quite clearly when the author cites mistakenly the work of Bettiza and O'Hagan (NOT O'Hagen) as contributing to debates about 'standards of civilizations', which they are not (on standards of civilizations and civilizing missions see for instance Gong or Bowden). Bettiza and O'Hagan are mostly concerned with visions of world order based on a plurality of civilizations. Again the two approaches to civilization/s in the plural and in the singular are not necessarily incompatible, for example some do speak about the West as one civilization among many others and also the most civilized among them, yet this is not exactly the type of claims which the paper is exploring. Or if it is, this should be made clearer.
Doing so is very important also because when we talk about the US these various understandings of civilization/s are present in different constituencies and expressed in different discourses at the same time. There is one tradition in America, namely American exceptionalism which the paper also refers to, that presents the US as its own particular 'civilization' distinct from Europe (certainly continental Europe, not necessarily Britain and the Anglo-sphere though) and distinct from the rest of the world. There is also, though, a tradition that positions the US as part of a larger Western civilization that includes Europe, seen as goes all the way back to Rome and Athens and simultaneously considers America as carrying forward this civilization's torch as the 'leader of the free world' and so on. Finally there is certainly a view of America as the emblem of modernity, as the liberal shining city upon a hill, which conjures visions of one great human civilization, (liberal) civilizational standards that all can emulate and reach, and dichotomies between civilized and uncivilized. Which of these visions is the paper referring to?

4) Conceptual clarity and precision.

There are major problems in the paper's use of the concepts of 'post-Westphalian' and 'postmodern', which I found were deployed in a very generic and loose manner. First and foremost, it was really unclear to me how either of these two 'post-' visions offered, as the author claims, "a third organizing principle beyond the conventional dyad in the modern discipline of International Relations (IR), that is, anarchy and hierarchy."
The paper suggests that China and America's Tianxian and Philadelphian visions of world order are 'postmodern'. Why and how? Let alone that such visions are retrieved from the past and therefore could be also described as 'modern' or even 'pre-modern' (in the case of Tianxia especially). But also, what exactly is postmodern about them? I was not ever really clear about that. What is furthermore problematic about such a label, is that it goes against the ways in which the US is often understood. I'm referring here, for instance, to Kagan and Cooper's discussions about the USA as a 'modern' power and Europe as the 'postmodern' entity, for instance. Of course Kagan and Cooper may be wrong, but if so, this needs to be explained better somewhere in the paper.
Second, in what ways are the visions proposed post-Westphalian? Now if we think of the Westphalian system as one centred on the concept of state sovereignty, then both the USA and China today quite jealously guard their sovereignty. Indeed these states find themselves reinforcing certain Westphalian sovereign principles against those actors who seek to undermine or transform them in the name of R2P norms or by wanting to strengthen global governance arrangements. Are America and China's visions leading to a post-Westphalian order because they are anti-imperialist? Yet this is not entirely convincing too, since both America's (ultimately) liberal vision and China's Tianxia have a distinctive hierarchical nature to them that mirrors in form – even if not necessarily in substance – older European forms of imperial relations (on this see also Ling 2010).
Finally, if we understand today's international order mostly as 'liberal' and 'American-led' (as Ikenberry, Acharya, and many others do), rather than in terms of Westphalian/post-Westphalian or modern/postmodern; then the big challenge here is not the one posed by America to the European way of doing things. In fact this has already been transformed. The challenge appears instead then one posed by China to the American (plus European) way of doing things internationally.

Reviewer: 2

Comments to the Author
It is a very interesting article. The comparison of the Philadelphian system and the Chinese Tianxia system reveals convincingly how major powers tend to rely on their own experiences and histories and ideas for rebuilding the world once their material capabilities allow. It is useful to warn that different world views of major powers may well lead to serious conflict. The world is going multiplex and the era of empire or hegemony is perhaps gone.

Just a few suggestions：

First, both the American and the Chinese views of world order is characterized as examples of exceptionalism. But exception to what? To the European view and practice? Isn't the European model exceptional? Perhaps it is more so, or at least equally exceptional. What should be cautioned against is to take the exception as universal, and take it for granted too.

Second, it is thus interesting to consider the relationship between practice and knowledge production. In some sense knowledge is always local in the very beginning. The European international system has produced the balance of power theory, the Philadelphian system and the Chinese tianxia system have also produced their knowledge. And these locally produced knowledge, once created, has strong impacts on practice.

Third, it is advisable to expand the reference list. For example, historically, the Chinese tributary system has been discussed a lot by both Chinese and Westeners (Fairbanks, Pye, and Dai Yi, just to name a few). Contemporarily, most of the Chinese scholars cited are leftist and new leftist. But many in fact strongly believe that a genuine global IR and an inclusive international system and society is the future of our world. However, this requires the reading of academic works published in Chinese.

Reviewer: 3

Comments to the Author
This article provides a comparative analysis of the type of world order envisaged by the United States and the People's Republic of China and focuses on the historical, cultural and philosophical underpinnings behind each country's worldview concerning the kind of world governance that ought to be seen. Given the growing scholarly literature in this area and the relevance of such comparison to our understanding of contemporary international relations, this article thus provides a timely and insightful contribution to our appreciation of the discursive dynamics at play. Hence, I am happy to commend this to the journal, albeit with some revisions.
1. The term "postmodern world order" occurs on several occasions (p1, 2), but it is not clear why the author sees the present world order as being "postmodern" and not simply post-Cold War as many IR scholars are wont to claim. To clarify what "postmodern" means, the author should attempt to include insights taken from sociology (David Lyon for instance) and other cultural studies (Nietzsche?) to explain the relevance of postmodernism (postmodernity) in the overall scheme of the discussion.
2. The author also buttress his discussion of American exceptionalism upon the scholarly insights taken from recent "international turn" in the studies of American political history, or what is term as a more "revisionist reading" of American political developments. While this is certainly useful from a scholarly perspective as it allow us to broader our horizon on American intellectual thinking, basing an entire section on an "international turn" without recourse to mainstream or more conventional approaches may be something that the author may want to reconsider. Recent historical works such as Andrew Preston's (Sword of Spirit, Shield of Faith, CUP) should be considered and where possible integrated into the broader argument the author wishes to make.
3. I appreciated the author's efforts in sketching out the broad parameters of the Project Tianxia and its relevance to Chinese exceptionalism. On whether the Tianxia order as a "postmodern alternative", I would like the author to elaborate further of how Tianxia and postmodernism is linked, or more specifically, what about the Tianxia system that is seen as being postmodern, and not simply Post-Western. This is linked to my earlier point 1 about the need to define and clarify the meaning of postmodernism as being applied to both the American and Chinese world order. Also, I am slightly puzzled concerning the illustration of the so-called new Qing History discussion (21-23) and its overall relevance to this piece. Is the author suggesting that this Qing History ought to be appropriated for the present, or is it just to highlight the fact that this is what happened and that Chinese leaders back then were more illuminated in their understanding and practice of "hybrid governmentality". In any case, I would be careful to perceive the Qing illustration as a idealized model that can be carried to the present (particularly given recent events in China and increased state power).
